English Grammar Foundations Q&A

August 2026  ·  Original notes based on ELLLO Grammar Talks & listening lessons

Notes distilled from ELLLO's "Grammar Talks" series and its beginner listening lessons — a run through the building blocks of English grammar: verb forms, pronouns, tense, and sentence structure.

Building Blocks: Words and Sentences

1.Why does the verb "to be" get taught before other verbs?

"Be" verbs (am/is/are) are used constantly for introductions, descriptions, and states — "I am," "she is" — and they behave irregularly compared to most verbs, so learning their forms early unblocks a huge range of basic sentences.

2.What role do adjectives and adverbs of intensity play in a sentence?

Adjectives describe nouns and adverbs describe verbs or adjectives, while intensity words like "very" or "quite" scale that description up or down. Together they let a sentence express degree, not just a plain fact.

3.What is the difference between subject pronouns, object pronouns, and demonstrative pronouns?

Subject pronouns (I, you, he) perform the action; object pronouns (me, you, him) receive it; demonstrative pronouns (this, that, these, those) point to something specific. Mixing these up is one of the most common early grammar mistakes.

4.Why do articles (a, an, the) cause trouble for many learners?

Many languages don't have direct equivalents to English articles, so learners have to internalize a new rule system: "a/an" for something non-specific and "the" for something specific or already mentioned, rather than translating word-for-word from their first language.

Tense and Time

5.Why is the present simple taught as a separate topic from the verb "to be"?

Most action verbs conjugate differently from "be" — they add "-s" for third person singular rather than changing form entirely (he "walks" vs. he "is"). Treating them separately avoids learners over-generalizing one pattern onto the other.

6.What is the difference between "was/were" and other past-tense forms?

"Was/were" are the past forms of "be" and describe states ("I was tired"), while regular past-tense verbs describe completed actions ("I walked"). Both are needed together to narrate a simple past event with both action and description.

7.Why does third-person-singular present tense need its own lesson?

English adds "-s" or "-es" only for he/she/it in the present simple ("she runs," "he watches"), which is an exception to the otherwise unchanged verb form used for I/you/we/they — a small but frequent source of errors.

Verb Types and Sentence Patterns

8.What is the practical difference between transitive and stative verbs?

Transitive verbs take a direct object ("she reads a book"), while stative verbs describe states rather than actions (know, believe, want) and are typically not used in continuous tenses — "I am knowing" is not standard English, unlike "I am reading."

9.Why does "there is / there are" get treated as its own grammar point?

This construction introduces existence rather than describing an already-known subject's action, and the verb agrees with the noun that follows it ("there is a book," "there are books") — a pattern that doesn't map directly from many other languages' phrasing.

10.Why do imperatives (commands) drop the subject?

An imperative addresses "you" implicitly — "Close the door" means "You close the door" — so English omits the subject pronoun by convention. Recognizing this prevents learners from assuming a sentence with no subject is incomplete or wrong.

Pronunciation

11.Why is English pronunciation considered especially difficult for learners?

English spelling doesn't map consistently to sound — the same letters can represent different sounds in different words (through, though, tough) — because the language absorbed vocabulary from many source languages without standardizing pronunciation rules to match.
