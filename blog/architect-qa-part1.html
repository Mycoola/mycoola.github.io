Salesforce Architect Interview Q&A — Part 1: Application Architecture

Updated August 2026  ·  Part 1 of 4  ·  25 questions
Salesforce Architect Interview Series (100 questions) Part 1: Application Architecture Part 2: Data Architecture & LDV Part 3: Sharing & Visibility Part 4: Integration Architecture

A general-purpose reference aligned to the Salesforce Application Architect certification domain and real-world architect interviews. This part covers core solution architecture responsibilities and platform capability trade-offs.

Architect Role & Responsibilities

1.What is the core responsibility of a Salesforce Application Architect?

Owning the end-to-end technical design of a solution — data model, security/sharing, integration approach, and platform feature selection — ensuring every decision aligns to long-term maintainability and Salesforce best practices, not just short-term delivery speed.

2.How does an Application Architect's role differ from a Technical Architect's or Solution Architect's?

Definitions vary by organization, but generally an Application Architect focuses on a single application/platform's design depth, a Solution Architect spans multiple systems and business context at a higher level, and a Technical Architect focuses on the underlying technical implementation standards and non-functional requirements across projects.

3.What artifacts should an architect produce during a project?

A data model diagram (ERD), a security/sharing design document, an integration architecture diagram, a list of key design decisions with rationale (architecture decision records), and a non-functional requirements checklist covering performance, scalability, and compliance.

4.What is an Architecture Decision Record (ADR) and why use one?

A short document capturing a specific architectural decision, the context/options considered, and the reasoning for the chosen approach. It prevents "why did we build it this way?" becoming lost institutional knowledge once the original architect moves to another project.

5.How do you balance long-term architectural purity against short-term delivery pressure?

Distinguish between decisions that are truly hard to reverse (data model, sharing model, integration patterns) — where it's worth pushing back on shortcuts — versus decisions that are cheap to change later (UI layout, some automation choices), where pragmatic short-term delivery is reasonable.

Multi-Org & Platform Strategy

6.What factors determine whether a company should use one org or multiple orgs?

Shared data/process needs favor a single org (unified reporting, one source of truth). Separate orgs may be justified by data residency/regulatory requirements, fundamentally incompatible business processes, or the technical need for org-level isolation (e.g., separate limits pools for very high-volume independent business units).

7.What is a hub-and-spoke org strategy?

A pattern where one central "hub" org holds shared master data (often via Salesforce Connect or MuleSoft) and multiple "spoke" orgs handle business-unit-specific processes, syncing back to the hub — used when full org consolidation isn't feasible but some unified view is still required.

8.What is Salesforce Connect and when would you recommend it?

A feature that lets Salesforce access data in an external system in real time via external objects, without physically copying/storing the data — recommended when you need Salesforce users to view/interact with external system data without a full data migration, and near-real-time accuracy matters more than offline availability.

9.When would you recommend a Salesforce package (managed or unlocked) versus building directly in the org?

Use a package when the functionality needs to be versioned, distributed across multiple orgs, or developed/tested in isolation from the rest of the org's metadata — an unlocked package for internal multi-org reuse, a managed package if you're distributing to external customers or need strict namespace protection.

10.How do you evaluate whether a requirement should be solved with a native Salesforce feature, a Flow, Apex, or an AppExchange package?

Work down a preference order: native/standard feature first (cheapest to maintain), then declarative (Flow), then a well-vetted AppExchange package if it closely matches the need without heavy customization, and Apex/custom code only when none of the above can meet the requirement.

Non-Functional Requirements

11.What are non-functional requirements and why do architects need to define them explicitly?

Requirements about how a system performs rather than what it does — performance, scalability, availability, security, and compliance. Left undefined, they default to whatever the implementation happens to produce, which often fails silently until the system is under real load or scrutiny.

12.How do you architect for scalability from day one without over-engineering?

Design for the data volumes and user counts the business realistically expects within a defined horizon (e.g., 2-3 years) based on stated growth plans, rather than either ignoring scale entirely or designing for an arbitrary "web-scale" that adds unnecessary complexity for a need that may never materialize.

13.What does high availability mean in a Salesforce context, and what's the architect's role in it?

Salesforce itself guarantees platform-level availability via its multi-tenant infrastructure and SLA; the architect's role is ensuring the solution's design (integration dependencies, custom code, external system reliance) doesn't introduce single points of failure that undermine that platform-level guarantee.

14.How do you architect a solution to be resilient to a downstream integration failure?

Design graceful degradation — if a real-time integration call fails, the core Salesforce transaction should still complete where business-safe to do so, with the failed integration logged and queued for retry rather than blocking the entire user transaction.

15.What compliance considerations does an architect need to factor into a data model design?

Data residency requirements (which region data must be stored in), field-level encryption needs for regulated data (PII, health, financial), retention/deletion policies (e.g., GDPR right to be forgotten), and audit trail requirements — these often constrain object/field design choices made early in the project.

Design Trade-offs

16.How do you evaluate a trade-off between declarative simplicity and custom code flexibility?

Weigh the maintenance cost of custom code (needs a developer to change, requires test coverage, harder for admins to troubleshoot) against the ceiling declarative tools hit for genuinely complex logic — the right answer depends on how likely the logic is to need frequent business-user-driven changes versus stable, complex rules.

17.What is architectural technical debt, and how does an architect manage it across a program of work?

Deliberate or accumulated design compromises that increase future cost of change. An architect manages it by maintaining visibility (a tracked backlog of known debt items), advocating for dedicated remediation time in roadmap planning, and preventing new debt through design review gates on major changes.

18.How do you handle a situation where the business wants a feature that's technically possible but architecturally risky?

Quantify the specific risk (performance degradation at scale, security exposure, upgrade fragility) in business terms, propose a lower-risk alternative that meets the same underlying need, and if the business still insists after understanding the trade-off, document the decision and implement the safest achievable version.

19.What is the role of a proof of concept in de-risking an architectural decision?

A POC validates a specific uncertain assumption (a novel integration pattern's feasibility, a performance-critical query's actual behavior at volume) cheaply, before committing significant budget to a full build based on an assumption that turns out to be wrong.

20.How do you future-proof an architecture against Salesforce platform changes (e.g., feature deprecations)?

Favor currently-recommended, actively-invested-in features (Flow over Process Builder, Lightning Web Components over Aura, GraphQL/composite APIs over older patterns), stay current with release notes for deprecation announcements, and avoid deep dependency on any feature explicitly flagged as legacy.

Communicating Architecture

21.How do you present a complex architecture decision to a non-technical steering committee?

Translate the decision into business risk/benefit terms (cost, timeline, risk of failure, future flexibility) with a simple visual, and avoid deep technical jargon — the committee needs enough to make an informed go/no-go decision, not a full technical education.

22.How do you ensure architectural decisions are actually followed during implementation, not just documented?

Build lightweight design review checkpoints into the development process (e.g., a peer/architect review before a significant pull request merges), rather than relying purely on documentation that developers may not revisit under delivery pressure.

23.How do you handle disagreement with another architect or senior technical stakeholder about a design decision?

Ground the disagreement in specific, evaluatable criteria (performance data, maintenance cost, alignment to documented non-functional requirements) rather than opinion, and be willing to be convinced by a stronger argument — architecture decisions should be won on merit, not seniority.

24.What's your approach to onboarding a new architect or senior developer onto an existing, complex org?

Walk them through the architecture decision records and data model diagram first (the "why"), not just the metadata itself, then pair them on a real, moderately complex change so they experience the org's actual conventions and constraints hands-on rather than only reading documentation.

25.How do you keep architecture documentation from becoming stale as the org evolves?

Treat significant documentation updates as a required part of the definition of done for any architecturally significant change, store diagrams in a format that's easy to update incrementally (not a static image nobody wants to redo), and periodically audit documentation against the live org during governance reviews.
