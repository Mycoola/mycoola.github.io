Salesforce Consultant Interview Q&A — Part 1: Discovery & Requirements

Updated August 2026  ·  Part 1 of 4  ·  25 questions

A general-purpose reference for Senior Consultant and pre-sales/solution-design interviews. This part covers discovery methodology, requirements gathering, and stakeholder engagement.

Discovery & Requirements Gathering

1.What is the purpose of a discovery workshop?

To understand the client's current business processes, pain points, and desired outcomes before proposing a solution — mapping "as-is" workflows to identify gaps, inefficiencies, and requirements that will drive the "to-be" design. Skipping discovery is the most common cause of solutions that are technically correct but don't fit the actual business.

2.How do you elicit requirements from non-technical stakeholders?

Ask about their process and pain points in their own language rather than Salesforce terminology — "walk me through what happens when a deal closes" instead of "how should the Opportunity object work." Use visual process maps and confirm understanding by playing back what you heard before translating it into technical requirements.

3.What's the difference between a business requirement and a technical requirement?

A business requirement describes an outcome or need in business language ("sales reps need to see a customer's full support history before a renewal call"). A technical requirement translates that into an implementable specification ("display related Cases on the Opportunity page layout, filtered to the last 12 months, sorted by date").

4.How do you prioritize competing requirements from multiple stakeholders?

Tie each requirement back to a measurable business outcome or KPI, then facilitate a prioritization exercise (e.g., MoSCoW — Must/Should/Could/Won't) with all stakeholders present so trade-offs are made collaboratively and transparently rather than unilaterally by the consultant.

5.What is a process map and why create one during discovery?

A visual diagram of a business process's steps, decision points, and handoffs between roles or systems. It surfaces edge cases and exceptions that get missed in narrative descriptions, and gives stakeholders something concrete to validate or correct before design begins.

6.How do you handle a client who says "just make it work like our old system"?

Dig into what specifically worked well for them functionally rather than replicating the old system's technical implementation — often what they actually value is a specific outcome (speed, a particular report, a familiar terminology) that can be delivered better within Salesforce's native capabilities rather than recreating legacy technical debt.

7.What is a fit-gap analysis?

A structured comparison of business requirements against out-of-the-box Salesforce capabilities, categorizing each requirement as a "fit" (native functionality covers it), a "gap" (requires configuration or customization), or "won't fit" (requires a workaround or process change) — used to scope the project and set expectations before design.

8.How do you write a good user story for a Salesforce feature?

Follow the "As a [role], I want [capability], so that [business value]" format, paired with clear, testable acceptance criteria. A good story is small enough to estimate and deliver within a sprint, and focuses on the "why" (business value) as much as the "what."

9.What questions should you ask before recommending a specific Salesforce Cloud (Sales, Service, Experience, etc.)?

Who are the end users (internal reps vs. external customers/partners)? What's the core process being automated (selling, case resolution, self-service)? What existing licenses does the client already own? Answering these prevents recommending an expensive Cloud when a cheaper, already-licensed feature set would suffice.

10.How do you scope a project when the client's requirements are still vague?

Time-box a discrete discovery/design phase as its own deliverable before committing to a fixed scope and price for build — this produces a requirements document and estimate the client can sign off on, rather than guessing at scope and absorbing the risk of scope creep later.

Stakeholder Management

11.How do you manage a stakeholder who keeps changing requirements mid-project?

Implement a lightweight change-control process: log the new requirement, assess its impact on timeline/budget/scope, and get explicit sign-off before implementing it. This isn't about blocking legitimate change — it's about making the trade-off visible so decisions are made deliberately, not accidentally.

12.How do you communicate technical constraints to a non-technical executive sponsor?

Frame constraints in terms of business risk and trade-offs rather than technical jargon — e.g., "this approach adds two weeks but reduces the risk of data loss during migration" rather than explaining governor limits or API rate limits directly.

13.What's your approach to running an effective status meeting with a client?

Come with a short, pre-shared agenda; lead with decisions/blockers that need the client's input rather than a full narrative of every task completed; and end every meeting with clear owners and dates for open items.

14.How do you handle a disagreement between two client stakeholders about how a feature should work?

Facilitate rather than arbitrate — bring both perspectives back to the underlying business outcome each is trying to achieve, and look for a design that satisfies both, or escalate to a single decision-maker with authority over that process if no compromise exists.

15.What is a RACI matrix and why use one on a Salesforce project?

A matrix defining who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed for each project decision or deliverable. On multi-stakeholder Salesforce projects it prevents delays caused by unclear decision-making authority, especially for sign-offs like data model or UAT approval.

16.How do you set realistic expectations about Salesforce's out-of-the-box limitations early in a project?

Address well-known constraints (e.g., standard object field limits, governor limits at scale, UI customization boundaries) during discovery rather than after building started, using concrete examples relevant to the client's use case so it doesn't feel abstract or theoretical.

17.How would you handle a client insisting on a solution you believe is technically unsound?

Present the risk clearly with a recommended alternative, document the discussion and the client's decision in writing, and implement the safest version of their request if they still choose to proceed after understanding the trade-offs — the goal is informed consent, not unilaterally overriding the client.

18.What is User Acceptance Testing (UAT) and what's your role in it as a consultant?

UAT is the phase where actual end users validate the built solution against real business scenarios before go-live. As a consultant, your role is to prepare clear test scripts tied to original requirements, triage reported issues (bug vs. new requirement vs. training gap), and track sign-off formally.

19.How do you handle scope creep during a fixed-price engagement?

Distinguish clarifications of existing scope (absorbed) from genuinely new requirements (logged as a change request with its own estimate and approval). Being consistent and transparent about this distinction from day one prevents relationship friction later.

20.What documentation should a consultant produce during a project, and for whom?

A requirements/design document for stakeholder sign-off, a technical specification for the build team, a data dictionary for anyone integrating with the org later, and an admin/end-user guide for the client's internal team to maintain the solution post-go-live.

Change Management & Adoption

21.Why do Salesforce projects fail even when the technical build is solid?

Most commonly: poor user adoption because change management and training were an afterthought, misaligned expectations from unclear requirements, or a data migration that undermined trust in the new system's data quality on day one.

22.How do you drive user adoption after go-live?

Involve end users early (not just executives) during design so the solution reflects their actual workflow, provide role-specific training rather than generic overviews, identify internal champions per team, and track adoption metrics (login frequency, feature usage) to catch drop-off early.

23.What is a hypercare period and why include one in a project plan?

A defined window immediately after go-live (typically 2-4 weeks) where the project team provides heightened, rapid support to resolve issues and reinforce training — preventing early friction from turning into long-term abandonment of the new system.

24.How do you measure whether a Salesforce implementation was successful?

Tie back to the original business case: adoption rate, the specific KPI the project was meant to move (e.g., reduced case resolution time, increased pipeline visibility), and user satisfaction — not just "the system was deployed on time and on budget," which measures delivery, not value.

25.How do you plan a phased rollout versus a big-bang go-live?

Choose phased rollout when risk needs to be contained (e.g., one region or business unit first) or when dependencies between features allow incremental value delivery. Choose big-bang when the process being replaced can't run in two parallel states (e.g., a single shared order management workflow) or when the org is small enough that phasing adds coordination overhead without reducing real risk.
